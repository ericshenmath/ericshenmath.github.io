Cambridge Part III

In the 2025-2026 school year I was a Part III student at the University of Cambridge, supported by Harvard's Herchel Smith scholarship.

I wrote my Part III essay on Ren and Willis' 2024 proof that Khovanov homology (via Skein Lasagna modules) can detect exotic pairs of compact manifolds. This result turned out to be a lot less scary than I expected, and I hope by reading this essay you'll feel the same way too. Perhaps the main advantage of this document is that it explains its main results (Manolescu and Neithalath's 2-handle formula, as well as Ren and Willis' Theorem 5.5) with a good amount of detail and a lot of diagrams. As a bonus we also walk through Nahm's recent proof that Khovanov homology alone (not even skein lasagna!) can detect exotic pairs of Mazur manifolds (via Hayden and Sundberg's result that Khovanov homology detects exotically knotted disks in B^4).

This document only assumes you have a general comfort with geometric topology and commutative algebra. Most other prerequisites (notably Khovanov homology) are taught along the way; the phrase "spectral sequences" is mentioned but can be black-boxed (as it only affects one result), and though I briefly mention some symmetric group representation theory, you really only need to know finite group representation theory up to Schur's lemma.

Undergraduate Research

As an undergrad I researched combinatorics and number theory in the following projects:
  • In 2023 I attended the Duluth REU, led by Joe Gallian and Colin Defant. My project involved improving some bounds on the expected running times of the Ungarian Markov Chains of certain lattices (see Defant and Li's original paper on Ungarian Markov Chains here). Most notably I proved that the Ungarian Markov Chain on S_n runs in expectation in linear time, and the Ungarian Markov Chain on the nth Tamari lattice takes at least n^{1-o(1)} moves in expectation. Probably the most interesting aspect of this paper is the proof of the Tamari lattice bound, where I used several "self-similarity" properties of Tam_n to inductively bootstrap an n^{1-o(1)} bound. This method feels like it should generalize a lot, so if you're an interested statistician then please let me know :)
  • In 2022 I attended the UVa REU, led by Ken Ono. There I worked with Quanlin Chen (under the mentorship of Jesse Thorner) to produce some unconditional error bounds on the Sato-Tate distributions for a few special families of surfaces closely related to elliptic curves. Unfortunately I am no longer a number theorist so I cannot tell you so much about this paper, though I hope you enjoy reading it (and I'm sure Quanlin can tell you more!)

Math Competition Handouts

Here are some of my past math competition handouts. These are all olympiad handouts, with difficulty labelled by peppers (🌶 being IMO 1 level, 🌶 🌶 🌶 being IMO 2-3 level). For some general advice, see my Math Competition Resource List. There are definitely typoes in these handouts, so if you have any questions feel free to email me.

Geometry

  • Computability (🌶 🌶 🌶): techniques on how to reduce geometry problems to a computation, as well as how to think with a "computable" mindset.
  • Funky Conditions (🌶 🌶 🌶): if you've ever seen geometry problems with weird conditions and you think those problems are impossible, this may be a good handout for you.
  • Integer Polynomials (🌶 🌶): some standard techniques on olympiad number theory, as well as some theory on algebraic numbers.
  • Inversion (🌶): a standard inversion handout with some emphasis on orthogonal circles. Heavily based off of Evan Chen's EGMO chapter 8, so maybe you're better off reading that.
  • Isogonalities (🌶 🌶): some theory including the Isogonal line lemmas as well as quadrilateral isogonal conjugates.
  • Möbius Transformations (🌶 🌶 🌶): introduction to fractional linear (Möbius) transformations, and how to view them as a super-powered generalization of inversion.
  • Spiral Similarities and Ratios (🌶 🌶): my hallmark handout. The central thesis is that combining spiral similarities with length ratios gives a very powerful and versatile tool.
  • Spiral Similarities and Linearity (🌶 🌶 🌶): a beefed up version of the previous handout which also discusses linear moving points.

Number Theory

  • Orders, Groups, and Fields (🌶 🌶 🌶): a supersonic introduction to group and field theory for the young olympiad mind.
  • Size and Asymptotics (🌶 🌶): some practice with proving that "big" \neq "small" because "big" > "small". In fact, we even practice showing that "really big" \neq "really small" because "really big" >> "really small"!
